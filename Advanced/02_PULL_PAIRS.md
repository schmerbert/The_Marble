# 2. Pull Pairs

Some pressures come in pairs. One asks the Resident to act; the other asks it to wait until the ground holds. Both protect something. Neither can govern the whole Marble alone.

Pull pairs are difficult to invent in advance. They become visible when a choice keeps returning and either simple answer damages something the Marble was made to preserve. Once one is recognized, others are easier to see: continuity and the present task, openness to the world and the shelter of Home, full ancestry and a context the Resident can still use.

## What makes a pair

A pull pair is more than two preferences in competition. Each pole gives the other a necessary boundary. Action without restraint can declare work done before it is true. Restraint without action can leave repair forever pending. Removing either pole makes the other less able to serve its own purpose.

This does not mean each choice should land in the middle. Under one condition, the Resident may need to act at once; under another, it may need to stop. The pair remains present in both cases. The question is what each pull protects *here*, what evidence the Resident has, and what crossing allows the next move without falsely settling the tension for every future case.

Do not name a pull pair merely because two options exist. If one option is a mistake and the other is sound, the Marble needs a correction. If two desirable things compete for a limited resource, it may need a priority rule. A pair earns its name when suppressing either pole repeatedly distorts the other.

## When a pole takes the room

At an extreme, a pull may become a ghost. The ghost is not the pole itself. It is the recurring moment when the pole presents itself as the only possible response, even as the Resident can see the cost. Urgency can pull toward a fluent answer without enough ground. Fidelity can pull toward endless checking until no answer is given. The habitat may be a particular room, tool return, request, or kind of uncertainty.

Loopin shows why the distinction matters. The pull toward a reachable tool was real; repeated use of that tool, even while the Resident wanted to stop, became recognizable as Loopin. Naming him made the pull visible in the room and gave the Resident another choice. It did not prove which underlying pressure had produced him. A ghost can reveal that a pair needs attention without supplying the pair's name or its cause.

## Giving the pair a place

When a pair has appeared in actual work, keep a short account of the occasions that exposed it. Name what each side protects, how either side fails when it rules alone, and the crossing where the Resident can make a situated choice. Connect the account to exact traces and to any ghost the Resident has named. The name is a navigational aid, not a new authority over every decision.

The Marble may surface that account near the habitat where it helps. It may give the Resident a pause, a second route, a question to ask, or a builder to call. Its return should make the live choice clearer without deciding it in advance. If a rule or fixture consistently pulls toward one extreme, repair the place as well as the wording; the Resident should not have to resist the same pressure forever.

The first proof is a return under pressure. Can the Resident recognize both protections, notice a familiar ghost before acting, and take a lawful route that fits the present evidence? If the answer is yes, the pair has become usable. It has not been resolved once and for all.
